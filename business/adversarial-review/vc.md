# VC: Try to prove this business would fail

> **Role brief:** you are an investor who has decided to pass. Write the memo proving
> why this fails. Argue in bad faith where the facts allow it, and in good faith where
> they don't.

---

## INVESTMENT MEMO — **PASS**

**Company:** NexaForge · **Sector:** K-12 EdTech, Nigeria · **Stage:** Pre-seed
**Recommendation: Pass.** Not venture-backable. **Also — and separately — likely to fail
on its own terms.**

---

## 1. The market is too small to matter

Let me do the arithmetic the deck avoids.

Their own analysis states **~14,000 addressable schools** at an average **₦81,000/year**.

> **₦14,000 × ₦81,000 = ₦1.13bn/year ≈ $750,000.**

**That is the entire Nigerian private-school SIS market at their pricing.** Not their
share — the whole market. If they achieved **100% market share of every addressable
private school in Nigeria**, they would build a business smaller than a mid-sized Lagos
restaurant group.

They know this, which is why the deck pivots to programmes and claims a ₦8–10bn
opportunity. But that requires a **completely different business** — one with facilitators,
curriculum, safeguarding, scheduling, physical delivery and parent collections. **That is
not software. That is a tutoring company with a CRM attached**, and tutoring companies
have low margins, high churn, geographic non-scalability and no defensibility.

**They are pitching a software valuation on a services business.** Pass on that basis
alone.

---

## 2. The central thesis is completely untested

Everything depends on **M3 — programme take-up.** Their own documents flag it as
untested.

**They have never sold a single programme. Not one.** No pilot, no waitlist with deposits,
no curriculum, no facilitator, no evidence.

And their model assumes **20–30% take-up at ₦2,500/term.** In a country where:
- Inflation has been running above 30% and real incomes are falling
- The naira has lost most of its value against the dollar in two years
- Parents are **already** struggling to pay school fees — the bursar in their own customer
  research says 180 of 620 students are in arrears

**Read that again.** Their own customer interview reveals that **29% of parents at a
target school cannot pay the school fees they already owe.** And the plan assumes 20–30%
of those same parents will pay ₦2,500 extra for an optional coding club.

**That is not a stretch assumption. It is a contradiction inside their own research.**

If take-up is 5% instead of 25%, programme revenue falls 80%, and their own model shows
software-only revenue never pays the founder a salary. **The business does not exist at
5%.**

---

## 3. The founder will run out of money before the model works

This is the most probable failure mode and the deck barely addresses it.

Their model: **~35 schools needed** to pay a ₦250,000/month founder salary from software
alone. At 3 schools/month with perfect execution, that is **12+ months.** With realistic
churn and seasonality, **18–24 months.**

**During which the founder earns nothing.**

They are relying on programmes to shorten this to 9 schools. But programmes are unproven
(§2), require a facilitator network that doesn't exist, need a curriculum that hasn't been
written, and demand safeguarding infrastructure they have never operated.

**The realistic sequence:**
- Month 1–3: 5 schools, ₦75,000/term revenue, founder earns ₦0
- Month 4–6: programme pilot, mixed results, founder earns ₦0
- Month 7–9: 8 schools, ₦120,000/term, founder's savings exhausted
- Month 10: founder accepts a ₦600,000/month remote engineering job
- Month 11–14: schools receive degrading support
- Month 15: schools churn. **Company dead.**

**This is the single most common death of the solo bootstrapped B2B startup**, and nothing
in the plan structurally prevents it. Their own risk register scores it 20 — the highest
number in the document. **They agree with me and are proceeding anyway.**

---

## 4. Founder-dependency makes this uninvestable regardless of outcome

- **One person.** No co-founder, no team, no bench.
- **Nine operational bodies**, all owned by the same individual. Three of them —
  programme delivery, data protection, safeguarding — are **explicitly unowned** in their
  own documentation.
- If the founder is ill for two weeks during results week, **every school loses their
  term simultaneously.**
- Every school relationship is personal and non-transferable.

**There is no company here. There is a person with software.** No acquirer buys that at a
premium; no investor underwrites it.

**And the honest structural point:** the plan's own moat argument depends on
*"founder credibility in cluster — cannot be bought, non-transferable."* **They are
describing their key asset as non-transferable. That is a definition of unfundability.**

---

## 5. Structural competition they cannot survive

Their own market analysis identifies the threat and then does not answer it.

**A payments company — Moniepoint, Opay, Flutterwave — will offer school management
software free**, monetising 0.5–1% of fee collections. Their own competitor memo shows the
economics: **₦900,000/year per school from the payment rail versus ₦81,000/year from
NexaForge's subscription. 11× the revenue, at a price of zero.**

And critically, from their own customer research: **the bursar — who controls spending —
has already been visited by a Moniepoint agent.** Not hypothetically. It has already
happened, at a target school, before NexaForge has closed a single deal.

Their answer is *"compete on programmes instead."* Which means: abandon the software
business (§1 already showed it's too small) and become a tutoring company (§2 shows it's
unvalidated). **Their defence against the competitive threat is to exit the market they've
built a product for.**

---

## 6. The marketing claims problem is a character question, not a copy problem

Their live marketing site claims:
- **"3× Award Winner"** — no award named anywhere
- **"20+ Active Schools", "500+ Students"** — implying 25 students per school, arithmetically
  absurd, and their own audit concedes these are unverified
- **Attendance tracking, advanced analytics, white-label branding** — features that do
  not exist in the codebase
- **Programmes, competitions, scholarships, government partnerships** — described in
  present tense, none delivered
- **14-day trial in one place, 30-day in another** — they don't know what they're selling

To their credit, they audited this themselves. **But it shipped.** Someone published
"3× Award Winner" on a commercial website with no award.

**In diligence, this is not a copy issue. It is a signal about how this founder handles
the gap between ambition and reality — and every subsequent number they give me now
carries a discount.** When the deck says "20% programme take-up," I have no basis to
believe it, because the same document-generating process produced "3× Award Winner."

---

## 7. Unit economics don't survive contact with reality

| Their claim | My correction |
|---|---|
| CAC ₦2,000–5,000 | **Cash** CAC. Real CAC is 20 founder-hours ≈ ₦100,000 at any reasonable opportunity cost. **20–50× their stated figure.** |
| Onboarding 6 hours | Their own engineer says 10–14. Student data arrives as *photographs of handwritten registers.* |
| 85% gross margin | Excludes founder labour, which is 100% of the delivery cost. |
| 85% termly renewal | **Guessed.** Never measured. Three renewal events/year means 85% termly = **61% annual retention.** |
| LTV ₦122,000 | Built on the guessed retention figure. Circular. |
| Programmes 50% margin | Assumes facilitators at ₦15,000/session with no evidence any exist at that price. |

**At 61% annual retention and a real CAC of ₦100,000, the LTV:CAC ratio approaches 1:1.**
That is not a business — it is a treadmill.

---

## 8. Even the success case doesn't clear the bar

Take their **best** scenario: 40 schools, ₦40M/year, 30% net margin → **₦12M/year profit
≈ $8,000/month.**

**That is a good outcome for one person. It is an irrelevant outcome for a venture fund.**
Nobody's portfolio needs a 3-year path to a $96,000/year profit stream with no exit and no
comparable acquirers in the market.

**Their most likely good exit** — acquisition by a payments company for the school
relationships — is precisely the scenario where the acquirer has already destroyed the
software business (§5) and is buying at a distressed price.

---

## 9. Where I'll concede ground

Intellectual honesty requires this section, and it is why I'm passing rather than
dismissing.

| Concession | Weight |
|---|---|
| **Offline-first sync is real and correctly prioritised.** It addresses the actual adoption killer in this market, and I have not seen a competitor do it. | Genuinely differentiated |
| **Term-based billing is smarter than incumbent monthly pricing.** They understand the customer's cash cycle better than better-funded rivals. | Real insight |
| **₦17,000/month cost base means they cannot be starved out.** My usual prediction — "they'll run out of money" — applies to the founder, not the company. | Unusual resilience |
| **The moat sequence is intellectually sound.** Time-dependent, order-dependent layers that capital cannot compress is a real strategic idea, better than most seed decks I read. | Credit where due |
| **Their own adversarial documents found most of my objections first.** The claims audit, the risk register, the Path A/Path B analysis. | Signal of unusual self-honesty |
| **Nine schools with programmes replaces a salary.** If M3 validates, the personal outcome arrives far faster than my §3 timeline assumes. | Materially weakens my strongest objection |

**And the deepest concession:** their business does not need me. It needs ₦176,000 and
thirteen weeks. **The absence of a funding requirement means my "pass" costs them
nothing** — which is, from their perspective, the correct design.

---

## 10. Conclusion

**Pass — on venture grounds, decisively.** The market is ~$750,000/year for the software
layer, the founder's key asset is explicitly non-transferable, and the pivot that makes
the numbers interesting turns a software company into a services company.

**But I want to be precise about the two different judgements here, because they diverge:**

**As a venture investment:** unfundable, and no amount of execution changes that. The
market size and the founder-dependency are structural.

**As a business:** genuinely uncertain, and I would not bet against it as confidently as
this memo's tone suggests. The cost base is near-zero, the product is built, the wedge is
real, and the founder has already written down most of the objections I would raise.

**The one thing that decides it is not in this memo and not in their deck.** It is whether
20% of parents at one school will pay ₦2,500 for a coding programme — a question that
costs one term and almost nothing to answer, and which they have not yet asked.

**My advice, which they did not request:** stop writing plans, stop building features,
and run that test this term. **If it clears 20%, you have a real business and you will
never need an investor. If it comes in under 10%, you have saved yourself two unpaid
years — and my §3 timeline was right.**

Either way, the answer is available in ninety days for less than ₦200,000. **Very few
founders I meet have a question that cheap and that decisive standing between them and
the truth.**
